Unit 1 — Introduction to Security

~10 class periods · Skills emphasized: Analyze Risk · Mitigate Risk · Detect Attacks
Unit foundation

How to Think Like a Cybersecurity Analyst

Use this framework throughout every Unit 1 scenario: identify what is valuable, what is weak, how it could be attacked, what could happen, how to reduce the risk, and what evidence would reveal an attack.

The cybersecurity chain

ConceptMeaningQuestion to ask
AssetSomething valuable that needs protection, such as an account, device, network, application, or data.What are we trying to protect?
VulnerabilityA weakness that could be exploited.What weakness makes the asset easier to compromise?
ThreatSomething capable of exploiting a vulnerability.Who or what could take advantage of the weakness?
AttackAn intentional action that exploits a vulnerability.How is the adversary actually trying to compromise the asset?
RiskThe potential for an attack to cause harm, considering likelihood and impact.How likely is the attack, and how serious would the result be?
MitigationAn action or security control that reduces risk.What can we change to make the attack less likely or less harmful?
DetectionMethods used to identify attacks or suspicious activity.What evidence would tell us an attack is happening?
IoCAn indicator of compromise: evidence suggesting malicious activity or that a system may have been compromised.What observable evidence should we investigate?

One example from beginning to end

A user's account contains sensitive information (asset). The user has a predictable password based on a pet's name and birth year (vulnerability). An adversary uses likely passwords against the live login portal (threat/attack). If successful, the adversary could access the account (risk).

A long, random, unique password and MFA reduce the risk (mitigation). Repeated failed logins followed by an unusual successful login provide evidence of the attack (detection / IoC).

Evidence is not the same as a conclusion

AP scenarios often give you evidence and ask what it supports. State the strongest conclusion that the evidence actually justifies, and do not assume details that were never shown.

A log showing 40 failed login attempts followed by one successful login strongly supports an online password attack. It does not prove whether the password came from phishing, guessing, password reuse, or another source unless the scenario provides that evidence.

Unit 1 analyst checklist

  • Risk: What is vulnerable, what threatens it, and what attack could exploit the weakness?
  • Mitigation: Which control directly addresses the vulnerability or attack mechanism?
  • Detection: What observable evidence would reveal suspicious activity?
  • Impact: What could the attacker gain, damage, expose, or prevent?
  • Evidence: What does the scenario actually prove, and what remains unknown?
